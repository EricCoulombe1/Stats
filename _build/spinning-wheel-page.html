The Spinning Wheel

Buy a rental. Let the tenants pay down the mortgage while the house grows in value. Borrow against that growth for the next down payment. Repeat, but only when the rules say it's safe.

In plain English

Instead of buying one house and only holding it, you refinance at renewal and use the money to buy the next one. On the same C$565,000 house used throughout this series, it adds about C$360,000 over 15 years when values grow and rents keep up. When they don't, the rules slow it down, and it ends up close to where simply holding would.

Where the money comes from

Every recycled dollar is either borrowed or earned. I report which.

Holding has a quiet problem. As the tenants pay down the loan and the house grows in value, equity piles up while the income grows only with rents, so your return on that equity falls every year. Refinancing puts the idle equity back to work. Here is what went back to work over 15 years in the base case, reinvesting.

Why moving a dollar can raise its return, and why not by as much as you'd think

A refinanced dollar costs the mortgage rate and, in the next house, earns roughly what houses earn. You keep the difference. Measured against what you first put in:

Return on your original cash ≈ return without moving + (amount moved ÷ original cash) × (what the next house earns − the mortgage rate)

Move C$50,000 out of a C$152,000 stake into a house earning 15% while borrowing at 5%, and your original cash earns about 3.3 points more a year (50 ÷ 152 × 10) for as long as that holds. In practice it holds only part of the time. Refinancing waits for renewals, every move costs closing and refinance fees, cash can sit idle until a house passes the purchase test, and when prices run ahead of rents no house does. In the model, the net effect over 15 years is about 2 points a year (16.5% vs 14.3% for holding), not the 30% figures promoted in some courses.

  • A downturn stalls it; it doesn't break itRefinancing stops for years and purchases wait, but nobody has to add money or sell into the fall. The coverage tests and the reserves are why.
  • When prices outrun rents, it stops buyingHouses get too expensive for their rent to pass the purchase test, so refinanced money sits in the bank earning less than it costs. Holding does slightly better. That's the rule working, not failing.
  • Rates matter more than growthAt 5.5% the conservative version can't find a house that passes the test, and it finishes a little behind holding. The aggressive version's 3-year cycle happened to fit one more purchase in, which is luck of timing, not a reason to push harder.
  • Pushing harder is a bet on good marketsRefinancing to 80% every 3 years adds about C$125K in the base case, carries the most debt in every scenario, and has the weakest result in the downturn when you're spending the cash flow.

How I run it

The rules that keep the wheel safe

  • LeverageBuy at 80% loan-to-value. Refinance to 75% at most (80% on the aggressive version), and never past what the coverage tests allow.
  • Purchase testNet rent covers the new mortgage 1.25 times at today's rate, or I add equity or pass.
  • Refinance testAfter refinancing, net rent still covers the payment 1.35 times, and at least 1.00 times if rates rise 2 points.
  • TimingRefinance only at a 5-year renewal, never mid-term, so there are no break penalties. Only if it frees more than the roughly C$4,000 it costs.
  • ReservesAbout C$15,000 per house, growing with values, topped up before any purchase. Shortfalls come from reserves first.
  • Stop switchRecycling stops if portfolio coverage falls below 1.25 times, five-year rates pass 7%, or local vacancy passes 8%. (An operating rule; the calculator models the coverage tests.)
  • Appraisal cushionLender value sets the loan. I plan refinances for a 10% lower appraisal and never count on refinance money for a deposit.
  • ReportingEvery recycled dollar is reported by source: tenants' paydown, growth in value, or cash flow.

What slows the wheel

Friction and risk, and what I do about each

  • Borrowing capacityBanks count only part of the rent, so room shrinks as you add houses. I build the lender ladder early: bank, credit union, monoline lender, then commercial or CMHC-insured debt.
  • Renewal shockA renewal at 7% instead of 5% can push a well-covered house below a lender's minimum. Staggered maturities and the refinance test's 2-point cushion are the defence.
  • Vacancy and repairsEnergy towns are cyclical. I stress-test at 8–10% vacancy and budget maintenance inside operating costs.
  • ConcentrationEvery house in one region tied to one industry is a real risk. Spreading across towns helps; it doesn't remove it.
  • Selling takes timeAn index fund sells in a day. A house takes months and costs about 3.3% to sell.
  • TaxAll figures are before tax. Refinance money is borrowed, so it isn't taxed. Selling triggers capital gains and recapture of depreciation claimed, while an index fund in a TFSA grows tax-free. Get your accountant's view on structure first.
  • ManagementMore houses means more decisions, lender work and oversight, even with local property management.
