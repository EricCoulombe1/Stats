Our own rentals

Our portfolio, by the numbers

My wife and I own rental properties in the same northern BC towns I recommend to clients. Here are two of them with the real figures: rent, costs, mortgage, cash flow and the yearly return on our own cash, including the months that didn't go to plan.

In plain English

Both properties pay us every month after every cost and the mortgage. Neither is counting on prices rising. Both are doing the job they were bought for: steady, positive cash flow that doesn't depend on prices rising.

A small, simple complex in a quiet part of town backing onto Crown land: no elevators, parkade or shared boiler to go wrong. Kitimat's rents are driven by the aluminum smelter and the LNG Canada build.

This is the wheel in practice. After a post-COVID jump in values we refinanced and put about C$50,000 of equity to work on our next purchase. The bigger mortgage didn't break it: it still pays us close to C$900 a month.

We hold this one for income, and the income has held.

Of our Fort St. John properties, this is the least flattering, and I picked it on purpose: it's the kind of house most clients buy, shown in an ordinary year with the costs that come with it.

The kind of property I look for: a newer house with a self-contained suite, so two rents share one roof, one lot and one mortgage. Separate meters and heating, extra soundproofing between floors.

2026 has been a real-world year. One month sat almost empty between tenants, and finding new ones cost C$3,000 in placement fees. Rent collected so far averages C$5,452 a month against a full C$6,000. It still paid us about C$690 a month after everything, and fully rented months have netted C$1,400 to C$1,900.

Both suites rent furnished, with utilities, internet and housekeeping included, so we pay those out of the rent. Over the last 12 months they cost about C$580 a month. Set them aside and the regular running costs (management, repairs, lawn care, property tax and insurance) came to about 30% of the remaining rent, right where my models put it. Adding this year's two re-renting fees brings it to 35%. What made the year look expensive was the gap between tenants, not the running costs.

At a 5% mortgage rate, the cash flow so far would be about C$390 a month: thinner, still positive. We value it at what we paid, with no growth assumed. The house wasn't bought to be sold soon, and the coverage tests I use are built for years like this one.

The bottom line: annual return (IRR)

What our dollars have earned here, and what they're likely to earn

IRR is the one number that counts everything: the down payment, buying costs and set-up, every month's cash flow, money taken out by refinancing, and what we'd walk away with after selling costs. It's the yearly return on our own cash, before income tax.

Estimates, not guarantees; see the notes below.

  • Kitimat's number isn't a forecast for anyoneWe bought in 2020 with a mortgage under 2.5%, and refinancing handed back most of our original cash within about two years. At today's rates that's hard to repeat.
  • Fort St. John is the realistic templateBought at 2024 prices and today's kind of rate. Held 10 years, it works out to roughly 14% to 17% a year before tax across the three value paths, in line with the model on the One House page.
  • The risk is timeNearly three years in, Fort St. John has returned only about 5% a year, because the costs of buying and selling weigh heavily in the early years. Returns like these need a long hold, cash reserves and no forced sale.
  • The other end of the rangeOur best performer, a four-door we bought in 2022, brings in rent after costs of about 10% of what we paid each year. It's the exception, not the plan.
  • What I'd expect for a dollar placed today (my opinion)Low-to-mid teens a year over a 10-year hold, before tax, with a real chance of a flat or negative first few years if you had to sell early.
