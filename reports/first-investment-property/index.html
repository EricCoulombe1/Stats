How remote ownership in Fort St. John actually works, what it costs, and the local team that runs it day to day.

In a nutshell

Owning a rental 1,200 km from home is very doable when three things are in place: a property bought on numbers that hold up in an ordinary year, a licensed local property manager who handles the day-to-day, and a cash reserve for the months that don't go to plan.

A good manager won't make every problem disappear, but it turns most of them into a phone call and a line on your monthly statement. Below, I walk you through a real year on one of our own houses, what management costs, what can still go wrong, and how I vet the people we rely on.

1 · The worries, named

Every first-time investor asks the same four questions

Almost everyone I work with starts in the same place. They like the idea of rent paying down a mortgage, and then the questions arrive. Each one has a working answer.

What if I pick the wrong tenant?

Screening is the manager's core job: a multi-point screening with credit and criminal checks, a written lease and a documented move-in inspection.

What if something breaks and I'm 1,200 km away?

A local manager with a trades network and a pre-approved repair limit can deal with an urgent problem the same day, without waiting for you.

What if I get the legal side wrong?

A licensed manager uses compliant tenancy agreements, serves notices properly and handles disputes through the Residential Tenancy Branch.

Can I trust someone else with this?

You get a monthly statement, an online owner portal and an inspection report every year. I read the same statements on my own properties every month.

None of these risks goes to zero. What changes is who deals with them, how quickly, and whether you hear about it as a crisis or as a line on a report.

Turnover is the real cost

One month sat almost empty between tenants, and two re-rentals cost C$3,000 in placement fees. Rent collected averaged C$5,452 against a full C$6,000. Fully rented months netted C$1,400 to C$1,900.

The fee is the predictable part

Management came to exactly 10% of rent collected. Setting aside the furnished extras, regular running costs (management, repairs, lawn care, property tax and insurance) were about 30% of rent, which is what my models assume.

It still paid us every month

About C$690 a month after everything. At a 5% mortgage rate it would have been about C$390: thinner, still positive.

4 · How Izon runs a property

Three phases, from keys to steady rent

Phase 1

Onboarding

  • A walk-through and a report on anything to fix before the first tenant moves in
  • Advice on furnishing where it pays, and where it doesn't
  • New locks, and the unit brought to Izon's standard
  • In my experience, set-up runs about C$5,000 to C$15,000 per property, depending on furnishing
Phase 2

Finding the right tenant

  • Listings on Facebook, Instagram, Kijiji, rental sites and through industry contacts
  • A waitlist of approved applicants that shortens vacancies
  • A multi-point screening that includes credit and criminal checks
  • Strong ties to the energy and construction employers behind Fort St. John's rental demand
Phase 3

Ongoing stewardship

  • Rent collected monthly, with a detailed owner statement
  • Maintenance handled through local plumbers, electricians and trades
  • A pre-approved repair limit, so urgent fixes don't wait for you
  • An annual inspection, plus move-in and move-out condition reports

Fort St. John went through a hard bust in 2015–16, and that history is part of why prices there still look reasonable. It's also why I insist on cash flow that works without any growth assumed.

7 · How I vet a property manager

Eight questions to ask before you sign with anyone

  1. Licensed? Look the brokerage up on the BC Financial Services Authority's public registry, including its discipline history.
  2. A sample owner statement. Is it clear what came in, what went out, and why?
  3. The full fee schedule in writing, including placement, lease renewal and any markup on repairs.
  4. The repair approval limit. What can they spend without calling you?
  5. Vacancy track record. How long do their units typically sit between tenants?
  6. Inspections. How often, and do you get the report and photos?
  7. After-hours coverage and the trades they rely on.
  8. Tenant reviews, not just owner reviews. Tenants who are treated well stay longer, and turnover is the biggest cost you'll face.
